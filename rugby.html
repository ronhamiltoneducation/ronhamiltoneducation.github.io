01 / Where it startedBrantford, Ontario → The world

It started with a possibility.

My first lesson from rugby: a shared passion can take you much further than you ever imagined.

I started playing rugby at twelve, growing up in Brantford, Ontario. By eighteen, it had already become something much bigger than a school sport.

At a provincial rugby dinner, I listened to a Canadian international speak about the places the game had taken him. His message was simple: rugby can take you anywhere. For a teenager who had barely travelled, those words stayed with me.

They turned out to be true. I played through university while also competing in varsity American football, and later found teams and friends in the Bahamas, England, Switzerland and beyond. The game took me to pitches across Europe, the Americas and Africa — and even into a match in New Zealand.

Some of my longest friendships grew out of those experiences. I still make the journey back to play for Millwall Rugby Club in East London, a club that has been part of my life for years.

Every pitch, every condition, the same team spirit.

Sport, young people and connection

03 / Rugby at school

From rugby to opportunity for everyone.

Contact rugby isn't a standard school sport for young children in Switzerland. Rather than leave the game out, I have adapted what I love about it through touch rugby: a non-contact format that puts movement, teamwork, decision-making and inclusion at the centre.

At Institut auf dem Rosenberg, I have coached and run touch rugby for pupils as young as six or seven, right through to eighteen-year-olds in the senior school sports programme. Different ages, confidence levels and sporting backgrounds can find a meaningful place in the game.

Beyond school, I am president of the St. Gallen Touch Rugby Club and play socially on Thursdays. That ongoing connection keeps me involved in the sport and in the wider community.

05 / Giving backThe connection to education

What happens on the pitch doesn't stay there.

“You see a different side of a young person when they discover what they're capable of.”

I've coached rugby whenever I've had the opportunity, at different levels and alongside my teaching career. A pupil who is quiet in class can discover a voice on a field. Someone who has struggled with setbacks can learn to reset, try again and trust the people around them.

Those experiences matter to me because they are not separate from education. They develop confidence, self-control, resilience and a sense of belonging — qualities that pupils carry back into lessons and into the rest of their lives.

Injuries have reduced how regularly I play contact rugby, but they haven't diminished my connection to the game. Coaching, touch rugby and community participation allow me to keep contributing in ways that matter.
